1 About melanoma  
Melanoma is a serious form of skin 
cancer. But it can be curable, especially 
if caught early. This patient guideline 
provides the latest information on 
melanoma testing, staging, and treatment 
to help you make informed decisions with 
your doctor. First, it’s important to know 
what exactly melanoma is.
What is melanoma?
Melanoma is one of the most serious types 
of skin cancer because it spreads beyond the 
skin more often than other skin cancers. But 
if discovered early, it’s curable with the right 
treatment. More than 3 in 4 are found early—
before they have spread—and when they're 
easier to treat.
Melanoma occurs when something goes 
wrong in your melanocytes. Melanocytes are 
skin cells that make melanin, a pigment that 
gives your skin its color. When skin cells are 
damaged, new cells may grow out of control 
and can form a mass of cancerous cells. For 
more information on where melanoma starts 
and how to prevent it, see the next chapter: 
Melanoma risk, prevention, and detection.
Diagnosing and treating melanoma early can 
lead to better results. It's also encouraging 
that more effective treatments have become 
available in the past decade for melanoma that 
has spread (metastasized).
Melanoma skin cancer
Melanoma usually has 
an irregular shape and 
different colors. It can 
show up as a change to an 
existing mole but also can 
appear as a new spot on the 
skin. See Chapter 2 to learn 
about the ABCDE rule.

Important note: While melanoma often occurs 
in people over 50 years of age, it can occur in 
anyone at any age—even children.
What are the signs of 
melanoma?
Melanoma spots (also called lesions or 
tumors) can be found anywhere on your body. 
Most often, they are found in areas of high-
intensity sun exposure, such as the trunk, legs, 
arms, and head. They may appear (develop) 
on normal skin or as a new mole or one that 
has changed.
Sometimes, melanoma can be found in areas 
that get little or no sun exposure and are called 
acral melanomas. These areas can include the 
soles of your feet, palms of your hands, and 
underneath your fingernails or toenails. People 
with darker skin are more likely to develop 
melanoma in areas that are less exposed to 
the sun.
Melanoma can also occur on internal mucosal 
surfaces, such as in the mouth, inside of the 
nose, on the tongue, in the intestines, and in 
the genital areas (mucosal melanoma); as well 
as in the eyes (ocular or uveal melanoma).
Although rare, melanoma can also show as 
a bump or a nodule that lacks dark pigment 
(usually pink or light brown in color), called 
amelanotic melanoma.
When you or your health care provider 
examines your skin, attention should be 
paid to the following:
  A mole that has changed in any way: size, 
shape, or color
  A new spot on the skin that persists, 
becomes raised, or bleeds easily
  Ugly duckling sign: a spot that looks 
different from other spots on your skin
To learn more about spotting melanoma on 
your own and about the ABCDE rule for moles, 
see Chapter 2: Melanoma risk, prevention, and 
detection.
How is melanoma treated?
Treatment for melanoma depends on the 
extent of the cancer, known as its stage. 
Staging of melanoma happens twice: after 
biopsy and after surgery. The reason for 2 
staging periods is because surgery often gives 
more specific information than the original 
biopsy. This means the melanoma could be 
changed to a higher stage (upstaged) and 
treated differently at that point.
There are 5 stages of melanoma that range 
from stage 0 (in situ) to stage 4 (metastatic 
melanoma). For more detailed information 
on staging, read Chapter 4: Staging for 
melanoma.

The lower the number, the earlier the stage 
and the easier the melanoma is to treat with 
fewer treatments needed. The good news 
is that most melanomas are found in earlier 
stages. And even if melanoma is diagnosed 
at a later stage, many potentially effective 
treatments are available.
Surgery is typically the primary treatment for 
earlier stages. To learn more, read Chapter 6: 
Treatment for stages 0 to 2: Early stage.
For melanoma that has spread regionally 
(locally), you might receive surgery along 
with other treatments, like immunotherapy, 
intralesional talimogene laherparepvec 
(T-VEC) injections, targeted therapy, or 
radiation therapy. For more information, read 
Chapter 7: Treatment for stage 3: Regional 
spread.
Sometimes melanoma can return, which 
is called recurrence. Recurrent melanoma 
is treated based on its pathological stage 
(assigned after surgery) and whether it has 
spread to another part of the body. You might 
receive treatment before surgery as well as 
after it. For more information on recurrence, 
read Chapter 8: Treatment for recurrence.
For stage 4 melanoma (metastatic or distant 
spread), you might have a combination 
of systemic treatment, surgery, radiation, 
intralesional T-VEC injections, or supportive 
care. Read Chapter 9: Treatment for stage 
4: Metastatic melanoma for more specific 
information.
How can I get the best care?
Advocate for yourself. You have an important 
role to play in your care. Many people feel 
more satisfied when they actively take part in 
planning their cancer care.
The NCCN Guidelines for Patients will help 
you play a larger role in your care. Discuss the 
recommendations in this guide with your care 
team. Ask questions about your options and 
share your goals and concerns.
Don’t know what to ask? You’re not alone. 
That’s why we include suggested questions to 
ask at the end of chapters.
Keep reading to find the best care for you.

2 Melanoma risk, prevention, and detection  
The last chapter gave a brief overview 
of melanoma and how this guide can 
help you. This chapter gives more details 
about the cancer, like where it starts and 
how to prevent and detect it.
To further understand melanoma, it’s important 
to know where it forms and what its risk factors 
are. With that information, you can aim for 
prevention and detection.
Where does melanoma start?
Your skin is your body’s largest organ. It 
covers about 20 square feet—about the size 
of an oversized beach towel. Skin protects you 
from invaders (such as bacteria, fungi, and 
viruses); helps control body temperature; and 
allows the sensations of touch, heat, and cold.
Skin has 3 layers:
  Epidermis – The outermost layer of skin 
provides a waterproof barrier and creates 
skin color.
  Dermis – This second layer holds 
connective tissue, blood and lymphatic 
vessels, hair follicles, and sweat glands.
  Hypodermis – This deep skin tissue is 
made of subcutaneous fat, connective 
tissue, and blood and lymphatic vessels.
Melanin
Located at the base of the epidermis are 
cells called melanocytes. These cells decide 
the color of your eyes and hair. Melanin is 
produced in these cells. This is the pigment 
(chemical) in skin that gives it color. In addition, 
melanin protects skin from harmful ultraviolet 
(UV) rays from the sun. When melanocytes 
grow uncontrollably, they start forming cancer 
known as melanoma. Melanocytes are found 
Where melanoma forms
Melanocytes are the 
cells located in the lower 
epidermis that, when 
damaged, can become 
melanoma.

in other areas of the body, but the focus of this 
guide is cancer of sun-exposed and non–sun-

Causes
It's unclear how cell damage leads to 
melanoma. But your genes, your health, and 
environmental risk factors can all contribute.
The clearest link is between exposure to UV 
radiation from the sun or tanning devices. 
Exposure to UV radiation causes changes to 
the DNA (genetic material) of pigment cells 
and increases the risk of melanoma, especially 
in people with lighter skin tones. It’s important 
to know that UV radiation does not cause all 
melanomas. Some melanomas can occur in 
areas of your body that are not exposed to 
sunlight.
What are the risk factors?
While the exact cause of melanoma is 
unknown, there are many risk factors that 
make it more likely that you will develop it. A 
risk factor increases the chance of developing 
cancer—it doesn’t mean that you will get 
it. Some risk factors are associated with 
activities like spending time in the sun or 
tanning devices. Other risk factors are genetic, 
meaning they are passed down through genes 
(from parent to child). For more information on 
genetic risk factors, see the next section.
Here are some of the most common risk 
factors that make it more likely for a person to 
develop melanoma.
  Lighter skin, hair, and eyes – If 
you have less melanin (pigment) in your 
skin, it means less protection from UV 
radiation. People with light hair and blue/
green eyes tend to have less melanin in 
their skin.
  A tendency to sunburn – If you 
sunburn easily or have a history of 
chronic sunburns, especially if you 
develop redness and blistering
  Many moles – If you have a high 
number of moles (more than 50) on 
your body or they’re large and unusually 
shaped
  UV light exposure – If you spend 
extended time exposed to UV light 
(radiation that comes from the sun or 
tanning devices)
  Earlier skin cancers – If you’ve had 
conditions such as actinic keratosis, or 
other skin cancers, such as basal cell or 
squamous cell skin cancers
  Childhood cancers – If you had cancer 
as a child, this could increase your risk of 
melanoma as you get older
  Weakened or suppressed immune 
systems – If you have a weakened 
immune system from organ transplants 
or autoimmune diseases, or you take 
medicine that suppresses immune 
function
  Rare inherited conditions – If you 
have a skin condition such as xeroderma 
pigmentosum, or certain hereditary breast 
and ovarian syndromes

Genetic risk
As mentioned, melanoma is a type of cancer 
that can run in families. About 1 in 10 people 
with melanoma have a family member who 
has or has had melanoma. Some families 
get melanoma because they share the same 
skin type or history of sun exposure. Other 
families share genes that put them at risk of 
melanoma. For more information on family 
history, see Chapter 3: Testing for melanoma.
Genes are the instructions in cells for making 
new cells and controlling how cells behave. An 
abnormal change in these instructions, called a 
mutation, may cause cells to grow and multiply 
out of control. Gene mutations can lead to 
genetic conditions like cancer.
Inherited genes that increase melanoma risk 
may also increase the risk of other cancers. 
You may have hereditary melanoma if you 
have a family history of:
  Skin (cutaneous) melanoma or eye 
(uveal) melanoma, especially among 
multiple blood relatives
  Pancreatic, kidney, gastrointestinal, 
and/or breast cancer
There are several abnormal (mutated) genes 
related to hereditary melanoma. They can 
include:
  CDKN2A mutation, which is the most 
common gene mutation for melanoma. 
It’s also called p16INK4A or MTS1. This 
mutation is often found in families where 
several members developed melanoma at 
a young age.
  CDK4 gene mutation, which significantly 
increases the risk of hereditary 
melanoma, but is considered rare.
  The melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1R) 
gene, which helps decide your skin’s 
color. MC1R can determine if you or 
your family members have red hair and/
or light skin, which increases your risk of 
melanoma.
  BAP1 gene mutations, which can 
cause uveal and, less often, cutaneous 
melanoma.
Genetic testing
Your clinical and family history might 
call for multigene panel testing. This can 
help your care team know if you have a 
higher genetic risk of melanoma and other 
cancers. This information can also help 
guide recommendations for early detection 
and monitoring in both you and your family 
members.
You might be referred to genetic counseling 
and testing if you or someone in your family 
has a history of 2 or more instances of invasive 
cutaneous (skin) melanoma. You might also 
be referred if you or someone in your family 
has a history of 2 or more non-skin cancers, 
like pancreatic, kidney, gastrointestinal and/
or breast cancer, especially in a first-degree 
relative.
Testing for other genes that can harbor 
melanoma-predisposing mutations may 
also be needed.

Preventing melanoma
Once you understand the risk factors, it’s 
important to know that you can take steps 
to decrease the likelihood of new melanoma 
cells from forming. Taking preventive action 
is especially important if you’re at higher 
risk, have sun sensitivity, or have a lighter 
complexion. Here are some of the leading 
ways to do that.
  Reduce UV (ultraviolet) radiation 
exposure – This means reducing your 
exposure to the sun and avoiding indoor 
tanning beds and other devices.
  Prevent sun damage – Limit your sun 
exposure between the peak hours of 
10:00 am and 4:00 pm.
  Wear protective clothing – This 
includes a wide-brim hat, clothes made 
with UV-protective fabric, and sunglasses.
  Use sunscreen – Choose a broad-
spectrum sunscreen with a sun protection 
factor (SPF) of at least 30. You should 
apply sunscreen 15 minutes before going 
outside to be most effective. Reapply at 
least once every 2 hours.
  Check your skin regularly – This 
includes both self-examinations and skin 
exams by a health care provider (see 
ABCDE rule next).
Limit time in the sun
Use caution when spending 
time outdoors. Reduce your 
risk of skin cancer by limiting 
sun exposure, wearing 
protective clothing, and using 
sunscreen.

Early detection: ABCDE rule
If you’re diagnosed with melanoma or if you’re 
trying to prevent another one from occurring, 
you’ll likely need to see your doctor on a 
regular schedule for skin checks (see Chapter 
10: Follow-up care). Between your regular 
visits, following the ABCDE rule is a good way 
to help detect the signs of melanoma. Check 
moles or spots (lesions) on your own in front 
of a mirror (a handheld mirror can help, too) or 
with a partner and keep this rule in mind:
  Asymmetry – This means the mole or 
spot is not uniform, and one half doesn’t 
match the other half.
  Border – The edges of a spot seem 
irregular and lack definition.
  Color – The color of the spot is not the 
same throughout (may be brown or black 
or sometimes with patches of pink, red, 
white, or blue).
  Diameter – The spot is larger than 6 mm 
across (about the size of a pencil eraser).
  Evolving – The spot or mole changes 
size, shape, or color over time.
Understanding melanoma like how it forms 
and what it looks like can help with preventing 
new melanoma lesions and those that come 
back, called recurrences. For more information 
on melanoma recurrence, see Chapter 8: 
Treatment for recurrence.
Early detection of 
melanoma: ABCDE rule
For skin self-exams, always 
follow the ABCDE rule to 
detect unusual moles.

Key points
  Melanoma forms in the skin’s top layer, 
the epidermis.
  Exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation is 
linked to melanoma.
  People with light hair and light eyes tend 
to have less melanin in their skin and are 
more at risk of melanoma.
  A risk factor means you are more likely to 
develop melanoma—it doesn’t mean you 
will.
  Reducing your exposure to UV light (time 
in the sun and tanning beds) is one way 
to prevent melanoma.
  You can help detect melanoma on your

  You might be referred to genetic

instances of cancer diagnoses in your 
family.
Questions to ask
  Does having risk factors for melanoma 
influence my chances of getting 
melanoma again?
  What number SPF is best for me?
  Can you recommend trusted brands of 
sunscreen?
  If I find a new spot or one that looks 
different than it did, what should I do?
  Do you have any recommendations for 
melanoma support groups?

melanoma, you might want to know about

diagnose melanoma.

3 Testing for melanoma  
You’ll need testing to correctly identify the 
type and stage of melanoma as well as 
to see if it has spread and how far. With 
that information, you and your provider 
will talk about treatment next steps. This 
chapter presents an overview of the tests 
you might receive and what to expect — 
keep in mind you may not receive all of 
these tests.
Basic health tests help your doctor detect 
diseases, including melanoma (see Guide 
1). Tests also help your care team judge the 
extent of the melanoma, which is called the 
cancer stage. You can read about melanoma 
cancer staging in more detail in the next 
chapter, Chapter 4: Staging for melanoma.
Medical history
A medical history is a record of all health 
issues and treatments you’ve had in your life. 
This information can help your doctor with 
treatment planning.
Be prepared to list any illnesses or injuries 
and when they happened. Bring a list of old 
and new medicines and any over-the-counter 
medicines, vitamins, or herbal supplements 
you take. Tell your doctor about any medical 
conditions or allergies you have. You should 
also tell your doctor if you’ve had any severe 
sunburns or other severe scarring in the past.
Your medical history also includes if you’ve 
had other types of skin cancer, such as basal 
cell skin cancer or squamous cell skin cancer. 
These are often less serious types of skin 
cancer.
More information on these skin cancers can 
be found in NCCN Guidelines for Patients: 
Basal Cell Skin Cancer and NCCN Guidelines 
for Patients: Squamous Cell Skin Cancer, 
available at NCCN.org/patientguidelines and 
on the NCCN Patient Guides for Cancer app.

Family history
Your doctor will ask about the health history of 
family members who are blood relatives. This 
information is called a family history. Some 
cancers and other diseases can run in families. 
Ask family members about their health issues 
like heart disease, cancer (including skin 
cancer), and diabetes, and at what age they 
were diagnosed. For more information on 
genetic risks, see Chapter 10: Follow-up care.
Physical exam
During a physical exam, a health care provider 
may:
  Check your temperature, blood pressure, 
pulse, and breathing rate
  Weigh you and measure your height
  Listen to your lungs and heart
  Look in your eyes, ears, nose, and throat
  Feel and apply pressure to parts of your 
body to see if organs are of normal size, 
are soft or hard, or cause pain when 
touched
  Feel for small structures called lymph 
nodes in your neck, underarm, and groin 
to see if they are bigger than normal
Skin exams
It’s important to have an experienced health 
care provider, such as a dermatologist, give 
you a thorough skin exam. Not only does your 
skin protect your body, but it also tells doctors 
a lot about your health. Providers take your 
pulse and blood pressure through your skin. 
They notice if the skin feels warm, hot, or cool 
to the touch.
Skin exam
A health care provider may 
use a dermatoscope (a special 
magnifying lens and light 
source held near the skin) to 
see spots on the skin more 
clearly and in more detail.

Skin exams are used for diagnosis, staging, 
and treatment planning. Expect a head-to-toe 
exam that includes a review of your:
  Head and scalp
  Face and mouth
  Eyes and eyelids
  Ears and earlobes
  Trunk, arms, and legs
  Hands and fingers
  Feet, toes, and toenails
Your doctor or other health care provider will 
look for spots that are unusual and need to be 
examined more closely or watched.
Lesions
Your provider will examine your skin for lesions 
(spots). A skin lesion is defined as a change 
in skin color or texture. Skin lesions can 
appear anywhere on your body. They can be 
benign (not cancer) or cancer. Your doctor or 
other health care provider may use the ugly 
duckling or the ABCDE rule (discussed in 
Chapter 2) and/or a specialized device called a 
dermatoscope to thoroughly review any marks 
or lesions on your skin.
When a melanoma lesion is diagnosed, 
it’s considered a cancerous tumor, 
so it will be referred to that way 
throughout this guide.
Skin color
Your skin color is mainly based on the amount 
of melanin in your skin. You know your skin 
better than anyone. Show your provider 
any changes or differences where it looks 
abnormal or different to you.
Pay attention to moles
More than 50 moles on your 
body and the presence of 
large or unusual moles 
increase the risk of melanoma.

Biopsy
If you have a skin lesion that looks odd to you 
or your doctor, part or all of it will be removed. 
This is called a skin biopsy. A biopsy is 
needed to diagnose melanoma. It will be sent 
to a pathologist who will examine it under a 
microscope.
Skin biopsy types
Skin biopsies are done using a local anesthetic 
(numbing medicine), which is injected into the 
area with a very small needle. You’ll likely feel 
a small prick and a little stinging sensation as 
the medicine is injected, but you should not 
feel any pain during the biopsy.
You may want to ask how the biopsy incision

An elliptical excisional biopsy removes an

For a punch biopsy, a small piece of skin and

This can help to avoid inaccurate pathology

Shave biopsy
A shave biopsy can be done either on the skin 
surface (superficial) or deeper. Superficial

is likely not cancerous. This type of biopsy is 
generally not recommended when melanoma 
is suspected, but may be useful for certain 
types of flat melanoma (mainly, lentigo 
maligna).
But deep shave biopsies (also called scoop

melanoma lesions to completely remove the 
suspicious lesion. This type of biopsy uses a 
tool similar to a razor to remove the top layer 
of skin (epidermis) and part of the underlying 
layer (dermis). The sample is sent to a lab to 
be examined.
Lymph node biopsy
A lymph node might be biopsied if your 
provider suspects it has cancer cells. Lymph 
nodes are small, bean-shaped filters of the 
immune system. Under normal circumstances, 
lymph nodes are usually too small to be seen 
or felt. But if a lymph node feels swollen, hard 
to the touch, or doesn't move when pushed (is 
fixed or immobile), it may be cancerous.
A lymph node biopsy can be done using a 
needle, which is preferred. It can also be done 
as a minor surgery to remove a lymph node, if 
it can't be biopsied with a needle.

type of your cells, as well as other features.

The dermal mitotic rate (DMR) or mitotic rate

The more the cells divide (higher mitotic

TNM staging
The American Joint Committee on Cancer

of cancer growth. The TNM stages will be 
combined to assign the cancer stage group.
  Tumor depth (T) refers to the thickness 
of the primary tumor and whether the 
tumor has broken through the skin like 
an open sore (this is called ulceration). 
Tumor depth is measured in millimeters 
(mm). There are 5 tumor designations 
(Tis, T1, T2, T3 and T4). See the Figure

whether the cancer has spread nearby 
(regionally) through small tubes called

satellite, in-transit, and/or microsatellite

the cancer has spread to parts of the

internal organs and distant lymph nodes.

Surgery
Surgery is an operation or procedure to 
remove cancer from the body. The type of 
surgery depends on the size, number of 
tumors, and their location. Surgery is the 
primary treatment for most melanomas.
A person diagnosed with melanoma should 
expect to have surgery after the skin biopsy.
The goal of surgery for a primary melanoma 
tumor is to remove all the cancer. For 
melanomas that have a low risk of spreading, 
surgery may be the only treatment needed. 
Different types of surgery are used to treat 
melanoma. They are mentioned ahead.

Wide excision
A wide excision surgery is the most common 
type of surgery. It removes the melanoma 
tumor as well as normal tissue surrounding 
it (the surgical margin). The surgical margin 
is measured in centimeters (cm). The size of 
the margin to be removed depends on the 
thickness of the primary tumor (see Guide 
4). Still, margins can be even smaller in places 
like the face, ears, palms of the hands, and 
soles of the feet because of their delicate nature 
and narrower surface area. In some cases, 
Mohs micrographic surgery (also called Mohs 
surgery) or staged excision with permanent 
sections may be used, especially in early-stage 
melanoma. For more information, see Chapter 
6: Treatment for stages 0 to 2: Early stage.
A wide excision may be done in a doctor's office, 
surgical center, or hospital (operating room).
You may receive local anesthesia before the 
surgery. Local anesthesia is medicine that 
numbs a small area of the body to minimize 
pain during the surgery. Most stage 0 and 
stage 1 melanomas are treated under local 
anesthesia.
For deeper or more advanced melanomas that 
require lymph node biopsy (described ahead) 
or more extensive surgery, general anesthesia 
may be used. You will be fully asleep during 
the surgery.
A wide excision is done even if the melanoma 
tumor was removed by biopsy. A wide excision 
also removes nearby microsatellites and lymph 
vessels in the skin because they could contain 
tumor cells.
A wide excision is often cut as an ellipse 
(football shape), which allows the wound to 
heal as a flat line. The surgical margin is cut 
based on the thickness of the melanoma 
tumor.

Side effects of wide excision
Side effects of wide excision surgery may 
include pain, swelling, numbness, and/
or scarring. Pain and swelling are usually 
temporary and should only last for a few weeks 
after surgery. Scars can be a lasting result of 
surgery. Talk to your doctor if you’re concerned 
about scarring after the surgery.
Following the surgery, a skin graft might be 
recommended for larger incisions. A skin graft 
is when a surgeon takes skin from another part 
of your body and uses it to cover the wound.
Skin grafts are surgeries that can be 
performed in the clinic or hospital. Most larger 
skin grafts involve sedation (giving medicine 
to help you relax) or general anesthesia, 
which means you’ll be asleep throughout the 
procedure and won’t feel any pain.
Sometimes, rotational “flaps” of tissue may 
be used to cover the wound so you can avoid 
a skin graft. A skin flap is a type of wound 
closure that takes skin from an area close to a 
wound and layers it to fill the removal of a skin 
lesion. Talk to your doctor about your options 
and how you can expect to heal after wide 
excision surgery.
Sentinel lymph node biopsy
A sentinel lymph node is the first lymph node 
where cancer cells are most likely to spread 
from a primary tumor on the skin. Sometimes 
there is more than one sentinel lymph node 
found, or multiple lymph node basins involved, 
like both armpit areas. The lymph node basin 
is the area of lymph nodes where lymph from 
the tumor drains.
A sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) is a 
surgery that removes one or more lymph 
nodes to examine for any cancer cells. These 
nodes will be checked by a pathologist in a lab. 
(Most people with melanoma have an SLNB at 
the same time as wide excision surgery.)

removes the melanoma 
tumor as well as some 
normal-looking tissue 
surrounding it.

Side effects of SLNB
You could experience some side effects 
after an SLNB, including numbness, pain, 
or bruising.
You could also develop a seroma, which is a

A seroma usually goes away on its own but

Your doctor or care team should discuss with

Lymph node dissection
A lymph node dissection is a surgical 
procedure where lymph nodes are removed 
and checked for cancer. The procedure may 
be done if the cancer has spread to the lymph 
node basin. But it is not often done if there is 
cancer only in sentinel lymph node(s). A lymph 
node dissection removes nodes to prevent 
cancer from coming back or spreading. This 
surgery is done under general anesthesia.
Complete (also known as completion) 
lymph node dissection (CLND) is a 
dissection done after tiny amounts of cancer 
are found in the SLNB. Although it can be an 
option for some people, nodal basin ultrasound 
or other imaging tests are more commonly 
used to check the lymph nodes.
Lymph node dissection is generally 
recommended for advanced melanomas when 
enlarged lymph nodes are found on clinical 
exam or through imaging. Or it's done for 
nodes that have grown over time and have 
shown to contain melanoma.
Lymph nodes
There are hundreds of small, bean-shaped 
structures throughout the human body 
called lymph nodes. Lymph nodes catch and 
filter out foreign particles and harmful cells, 
including cancer cells.

Order of Treatments
Most people with melanoma will receive 
more than one type of treatment. This is 
an overview of the order of treatments 
and what they do.
Neoadjuvant (before) treatment is 
given to shrink the tumor before primary 
treatment (surgery). This might make 
surgery possible for some challenging 
melanoma tumors.
Primary treatment is the main treatment 
given to rid the body of cancer. Surgery 
is usually the main treatment when the 
tumor can be removed, often in early-
stage melanoma. In later stages, surgery 
is often combined with cancer drugs.
• First-line treatment is the first set of 
cancer drugs given.
• Second-line treatment is the next set 
of cancer drugs given if the first-line 
treatment hasn't worked.
Adjuvant (after) treatment is given after 
primary treatment to rid the body of any 
cancer cells left behind from surgery. 
It's also used when the risk of cancer 
returning (recurrence) is felt to be high.
Talk with your doctor about your treatment 
plan and what it means for your stage of 
melanoma.

Systemic therapy
Systemic therapies are cancer drugs that 
travel throughout the body and are often used 
to treat more advanced melanoma. Depending 
on the stage, systemic therapies could be 
used as neoadjuvant treatments (before the 
primary treatment, which is often surgery) or 
adjuvant treatments (after the primary surgery 
treatment).
The most common types of systemic therapies 
that treat melanoma include targeted therapies 
and immunotherapies, and, less often, 
chemotherapies. Each type of treatment is 
explained below.
When it comes to systemic therapies, 
all treatments listed in this guide are 
recommended and appropriate. When 
helpful, NCCN experts also assign a level 
of preference to their recommendations for 
systemic therapies:
  Preferred therapies have the most 
evidence they may work better and be 
safer than other therapies.
  Other recommended therapies 
can provide effective results but may 
have less evidence, more side effects, or 
may not work quite as well as preferred 
therapies.
  Therapies used in certain cases 
work best for individuals with specific 
cancer features or health circumstances.
Targeted therapy
Targeted therapy drugs are a type of systemic 
therapy designed to target and attack specific 
cancer cells. For melanoma, these drugs 
target the activity of a specific or unique 
feature of melanoma cells and interfere with 
how those cancer cells grow.
Cancer growth inhibitors
Cancer growth inhibitors (or blockers) are a 
type of targeted therapy that stops cancer cells 
from growing. Our bodies make chemicals 
called growth factors that control cell growth.
Cancer growth inhibitors work by blocking 
the growth factors that trigger cancer cells to 
divide and grow. They are often taken orally 
(by mouth) and can be taken at home.
There are several types of cancer growth 
inhibitors, including the following.

directly attack these proteins. They can 
shrink or slow the growth of tumors in

removed completely. Types of BRAF 
inhibitors include vemurafenib (Zelboraf), 
dabrafenib (Tafinlar), and encorafenib 
(Braftovi).
  MEK inhibitors: MEK is a protein that's 
activated when the BRAF gene mutates 
and causes melanoma cancer cells to 
grow. Drugs known as MEK inhibitors 
target and attack the MEK protein.

Immunotherapy
The immune system is the body's defense 
against infection and disease. Immunotherapy 
is a systemic treatment that uses the immune 
system to kill cancer cells.
Your immune system can tell the difference 
between normal cells and those it sees as 
foreign, like germs and cancer cells. And 
for the most part, it will do its job to fight the 
foreign cells but not the normal cells.
T cells are a key part of the immune system. 
T cells that kill infected cells and cancer cells 
are called cytotoxic or killer T cells.
But sometimes the proteins on tumor cells bind 
with T-cell proteins, which prevents the T cells 
from killing the tumor cells. These tumor 
cell proteins are called immune checkpoint 
proteins. PD-L1 is a tumor cell checkpoint 
protein that can bind with PD-1 checkpoint 
protein on T cells.
Immune checkpoint inhibitors
Immune checkpoint inhibitors are a type of 
immunotherapy. They work by blocking the 
proteins from binding, which then frees the 
T cells to kill the tumor cells.
Checkpoint inhibitors are slowly injected into 
a vein (infusion). It may take up to 30 to 60 
minutes to get the full dose. Infusions are 
usually given every few weeks. The number of 
weeks between treatments can depend on the 
type of inhibitor used.

Interleukin 2 (IL-2, Proleukin) is an

supercharging the immune system to target 
and kill cancer cells.

injected directly into the tumor. T-VEC destroys 
tumor cells and also helps your body find and 
attack nearby cancer cells. Some side effects 
of T-VEC include fever, headaches, chills, 
muscle aches, and feeling tired and weak. 
Most people—about 9 out of 10—who receive

usually get better after a few days.

TIL
Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy 
(Lifileucel, Amtagvi) is an immune cell 
treatment approved as a second-line therapy 
for people whose melanoma hasn’t responded 
to other immunotherapies. It uses the person's 
own cancer-fighting T cells, called tumor-
infiltrating lymphocytes. These cells are 
collected from a person's own tumor. In a lab, 
the TILs are isolated from the tumor sample, 
grown into the billions, and infused (through an 
IV) back into the person.
TIL is used for melanoma that has a BRAF 
mutation and has progressed on prior 
immunotherapy and targeted therapy. It’s a 
specialized type of cellular therapy given only 
in approved centers with experience in the 
technique.
Side effects are common and come from the 
chemotherapy (see next section) given along 
with the TIL. They include anemia, high fever, 
and drops in platelets and certain white blood 
cells. When used with IL-2, see side effects 
of IL-2 discussed on page 45. Side effects 
usually resolve within a couple of weeks.

Chemotherapy kills fast-growing cells 
throughout the body, including both cancer 
cells and normal cells. Chemotherapy drugs 
are usually given through an injection into a 
vein (IV) or are taken as a pill (by mouth).

melanoma as well as immunotherapy and 
targeted therapy do, your care team will likely 
use those therapies first.

immunotherapy or targeted therapy or if you 
have severe side effects, chemotherapy may 
be a possibility.

drugs are given through an

Radiation therapy
Radiation therapy is a painless treatment that 
focuses high-energy rays on tumor cells. The 
rays are delivered to the tumor area to damage 
the DNA inside of the tumor cells. This either 
kills the tumor cells or stops new tumor cells 
from being made. You won’t see, hear, or feel 
the radiation. It passes through your skin and 
other tissues to reach the tumor.
Radiation therapy can be given alone or with 
other treatments. Treatment may focus on 
individual tumors, a small area of the body, or 
a specific lymph node area. Radiation therapy 
can also be used as a palliative treatment to 
help ease the pain or discomfort caused by 
cancer.
External beam radiation therapy
External beam radiation therapy (EBRT) is 
the most commonly used method of radiation 
therapy. Radiation beams from a large 
machine are directed at the tumor. The beams 
overlap at the site of the tumor or cancer cells 
to target a high dose of radiation to that area. 
A much lower dose is given to nearby tissue. 
How often the treatment is given is based 
on the goal of your treatment and where the 
melanoma is located.
Intensity-modulated radiation 
therapy
Intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) 
uses many small beams of different strengths. 
This allows a high dose of radiation to be 
targeted at the tumor while limiting the amount 
of radiation to the surrounding normal tissue.

6 Treatment for stages 0 to 2: Early stage  
This chapter explains the early stages of 
melanoma—when most melanomas are 
diagnosed. It provides an overview of the 
best course of treatment and follow-up care.
The early stages of melanoma, especially 
stages 0 and 1, are when the cancer is highly 
treatable and curable. After your biopsy 
confirms melanoma and its stage, your doctor 
will do a physical exam and medical history. 
You’ll only need imaging tests if you have 
any unusual symptoms or if it might help with 
planning for surgery or systemic therapy.
Keep reading to find out more about how the 
melanoma's stage reflects what treatment you 
may receive. See Guide 5.
Stage 0 in situ
Stage 0 in situ refers to melanoma found 
only in the top layer of skin (epidermis). The 
melanoma at this stage is very unlikely to 
spread to other parts of the body. It’s cured 
when completely removed.
Treatment
The preferred treatment for stage 0 in situ is 
wide excision surgery. Wide excision surgery 
removes the melanoma tumor as well as 
some normal-looking tissue surrounding it (the 
surgical margin). Some patients with stage 0 in 
situ may be offered nonsurgical treatment with 
the immunotherapy cream imiquimod (5%) 
or radiation therapy. Still, depending on your 
health and ability to have surgery, you might 
not have any treatment but instead will be 
closely observed by your doctor.
But surgery is the most common treatment for 
stage 0 in situ. There are some areas where 
it’s harder to do surgery, such as on the face, 
ears, palms of the hands, and soles of the feet. 
In these cases, your doctor might consider 
alternative surgical techniques, like Mohs 
micrographic surgery (also called Mohs surgery) 
or staged excision with permanent sections.
Both types of surgery can provide a closer

  Less than 0.8 mm thick with ulceration
  Between 0.8 and 1 mm thick with or

  Between 1 and 2 mm thick without

7 Treatment for stage 3: Regional spread  
Stage 3 melanoma is considered an 
advanced form of cancer. In this stage, 
the melanoma has spread to the nearby 
lymph nodes, lymph vessels, or other 
parts of the skin but has not spread 
further than that. There are many 
treatment options depending on the 
features of the melanoma.
Stage 3 melanoma means the cancer has 
spread from the main skin tumor to nearby 
areas. This is called regional melanoma 
because the cancer has spread locally but not 
to distant parts of the body.
Features of regional spread
Stage 3 melanoma has 1 or more of these 4 
features:
  Nearby lymph nodes with cancer 
may be found. (When distant nodes have 
cancer, the cancer is stage 4.)
  Microscopic satellites 
(microsatellites) are tiny skin tumors 
that are next to or below the main skin 
tumor and can be seen only with a 
microscope.
  Satellite tumors are deposits of 
melanoma in the lymph vessels no more 
than 2 cm from the main tumor. They are 
large enough to be seen or felt during a 
skin exam.
  In-transit tumors are lymph vessel 
deposits that are further than 2 cm from 
the main tumor but haven’t reached the 
lymph nodes.
Satellite and in-transit tumors
Melanoma cells can spread from the main tumor through the lymph system. This system 
consists of small vessels and bean-shaped structures called lymph nodes (shown in 
green). Melanoma may form one or more satellite tumors, which are close to the main 
tumor. In-transit tumors are farther away but haven’t reached nearby lymph nodes.

Stage 3 cancer is further broken down into

chapter is organized based on the treatment

Stage 3A
Stage 3A melanoma includes tumors that may 
be:
  1 mm thick or less with or without

  2 mm thick or less without ulceration

Stage 3B

  Thickness less than 1 mm or up to 4 mm

  1 to 3 lymph nodes found to have cancer

  Microsatellite, satellite, or in-transit tumors

Stage 3C
A stage 3C tumor is identified as:
  Less than 2 mm thick to no more than 4

  Involving anywhere from 1 to 4 lymph

tests, and/or any number of lymph nodes 
clumped (matted) together
  Having microsatellite, satellite, or in-

Stage 3D

  More than 4 mm thick with ulceration
  Found with cancer in 4 or more lymph 
nodes or any number of lymph nodes 
clumped (matted) together
  Associated with 2 or more lymph nodes 
and/or any number of lymph nodes 
clumped together. There are also 
microsatellite, satellite, and/or in-transit 
tumors on or under the skin

Cancer in the sentinel nodes

Tests
For stage 3A melanoma, your care team 
may consider imaging to help with staging.

8 Treatment for recurrence  
When cancer comes back after treatment, 
it's called recurrent cancer. This chapter 
reviews the different types of melanoma 
recurrence as well as testing and 
treatment options.
When melanoma recurs, it often doesn't look 
like the original melanoma. Many times, the 
melanoma appears as lumps under the skin or 
in the lymph nodes. Early-stage melanoma is 
less likely to recur than advanced melanoma. 
Most recurrences happen within 3 years 
after treatment. If the recurrence is a distant 
metastasis, read Chapter 9: Treatment for 
stage 4: Metastatic melanoma for options.
True scar recurrence
True scar recurrence, also known as persistent 
cancer, is a tumor next to the scar tissue from 
the melanoma surgery. It occurs because 
not all of the melanoma was removed during 
surgery despite best efforts.
This tumor looks like the original melanoma. 
It’s often found in the top layers of the skin 
(epidermis or superficial dermis).
Tests
Testing for true scar recurrence includes a 
skin biopsy to confirm it’s cancer. The next 
tests you’ll receive are based on the stage 
and features of the recurrent melanoma. Tests 
could include lymphatic mapping (the use of 
dyes and radioactive substances to find cancer 
in lymph nodes) or sentinel lymph node biopsy 
(SLNB). Ask your care team about any other 
testing you may have.
Treatment
Treatment options for true scar recurrence are 
listed in Guide 12. They include:
  Surgery to remove the tumor, with 
surgical margins based on the tumor 
depth (Breslow thickness)
  SLNB based on lymphatic mapping if the 
tumor grew near lymph vessels
Depending on how advanced the recurrence 
is, your care team might also recommend:
  Observation with or without surveillance 
imaging
  Pembrolizumab or nivolumab for 
pathologically staged 2B or 2C
  Primary tumor site radiation therapy

9 Treatment for stage 4: Metastatic melanoma  
Stage 4 cancer, also called metastatic 
cancer, is melanoma that has spread 
far from where it started. Metastatic 
melanoma most often spreads to distant 
lymph nodes, the brain, bones, liver, 
lungs, or gastrointestinal (GI) tract.
It’s important to know that stage 4 melanoma is 
treatable (Guide 15). Find a care team that's 
experienced in treating the type and stage of 
cancer and explore clinical trial options. Always 
ask questions. This increases your chances of 
getting the best possible outcome.
Metastatic melanoma may be treated with 
multiple lines of systemic therapy. The first 
treatment given is referred to as first-line 
therapy. Second-line therapy is the second 
treatment used. If more lines of therapy are 
needed, other second-line options may be 
tried. See Guide 16 for systemic therapy 
options for metastatic melanoma.
Preferred regimens work better, are safer, or 
cost less than other options. Your medical 
oncologist may not prescribe a preferred 
regimen. Ask why because there are different 
reasons for this decision.
Non-preferred regimens also have benefits for 
people with melanoma. They may extend life, 
delay cancer growth, or reduce symptoms.
Be sure to ask your care team if you have 
questions about any of your options.

melanoma that has spread to many distant 
sites and/or to the brain as brain metastases. 
It’s unresectable, which means it cannot be 
completely removed with surgery.
Treatment
The first step in treating widely spread 
metastases is to learn if the cancer has 
reached your brain. Brain metastases are 
often treated first to prevent serious health 
problems. Your care team will meet with 
you to discuss treatment options.
Preferred treatment for widely spread and/or 
brain metastases includes systemic therapy 
listed in Guide 16. For skin lesions that have 
spread to other skin sites, T-VEC injections are 
a treatment option.
Otherwise, surgery is an option if large brain 
metastases are causing symptoms or if 
there is a single metastasis—this is called 
a palliative resection (surgery) because it’s 
intended to relieve symptoms vs. removing the 
cancer (discussed further below).
Or you may receive radiation therapy after 
surgery. The preferred radiation method is 
stereotactic ablative radiation therapy (SBRT). 
This is therapy that uses a high dose of 
radiation directly into the tumor. If SBRT is not 
an option, whole brain radiation therapy may 
be considered.
Sometimes, brain metastases are treated 
with the goal of relieving symptoms and not 
removing the cancer. This is called palliative 
care. Palliative options for brain metastases 
include:
  Corticosteroids to relieve swelling
  Anticonvulsant therapy to control seizures
  Bevacizumab to help with quality of life
To summarize, treatment options for widely 
spread and/or brain metastases include:
  Systemic therapy (preferred)
  Palliative surgery
  Radiation therapy
  T-VEC injections to treat metastatic skin 
lesions
  Best supportive/palliative care

Ask for help
Cancer and its treatment can be very 
overwhelming. Getting help when you’re 
feeling worried or hopeless is an important part 
of cancer care. And if you have a caregiver, 
this could apply to them, too. If you’re both 
feeling anxious or overwhelmed, ask your 
care team for help.
Part of asking for help could also mean talking 
to other people who have the same type of 
cancer as you. Often the best advice comes 
from others who’ve been there. And dozens 
of online and in-person support groups are 
available where you can share your feelings 
and worries or anything on your mind.
Most cancer centers have patient-to-patient 
peer support groups. Don’t hesitate to ask your 
care team (like a social worker or a behavioral 
health expert) to recommend support groups, 
or do an online search of groups that meet 
near you or others that might meet virtually.
If you’re looking for spiritual help and 
guidance, ask your care team about any 
resources for that, too.
It’s normal to feel worried
Depression, anxiety, fear, and distress are very common feelings in 
people with cancer. These feelings can make it harder to deal with 
cancer and cancer treatment. They can hold you back even when 
you want to move forward. Getting help when you’re feeling worried 
is an important part of cancer care. If you’re feeling anxious or 
overwhelmed, ask your treatment team for help.
Read more about cancer and distress in NCCN Guidelines for 
Patients: Distress During Cancer Care, available at NCCN.org/
patientguidelines and on the NCCN Patient Guides for Cancer app.

10 Follow-up care  
After treatment for melanoma, your care 
team will want to check you for any new 
skin cancers—often called follow-up, 
observation, surveillance, or monitoring. 
This chapter briefly explains ongoing care 
for everyone who’s had melanoma.
You’ve gone through your melanoma 
treatment, so the next step is attending regular 
visits with your doctor to look for any new 
lesions and to make sure the melanoma hasn’t 
spread.
Melanoma tests
You may receive more than one type of test for 
melanoma in follow-up care.
Skin and lymph node exams
Your provider may examine your skin 
and lymph nodes at least once a year, 
depending on the melanoma stage. Your 
doctor should also explain how you can do 
self-exams of your skin and lymph nodes at 
home. It’s important to regularly look for any 
new, changing, or unusual spots on both 
sun-exposed and non–sun-exposed skin.
What you can do:
  Examine your skin regularly—If you spot 
anything that seems out of the ordinary 
or doesn’t look normal, have it checked 
as soon as possible. See Chapter 2 for 
detection information.
  Visit your health care provider annually 
and ask for a full-body skin exam by a 
skin care provider, like a dermatologist, 
at least once a year. You may want to 
consider a skin exam more often if you’re 
at higher risk of skin cancer.
Skin devices
Your care team might use medical tools to 
check for skin cancer, such as:
  A dermatoscope—a handheld device to 
provide a closer look at suspicious lesions
It’s good practice to look at 
your skin for anything unusual 
at least once a month.

Imaging tests
If you had a lymph node exam that showed

doctor feels it’s needed.

return. This can depend on your family history 
of melanoma, if you have a high mole count, or 
if you have any atypical-looking moles.
Be your own detector

spots on your own, too. Your doctor will likely 
encourage you to do so as part of your follow-
up care about once a month. Be sure to read

prevention for ways to do that.

Words to know
Words to know
ABCDE rule
A memory device to recognize the 
characteristics of moles or skin lesions that 
might be cancer.
adjuvant treatment
Treatment (usually medicine or radiation) given 
after the main (primary) treatment.
advanced melanoma
Cancer that has spread beyond the area near 
the main tumor.
anesthesia
A drug or other substance that causes a 
controlled loss of feeling or awareness with or 
without loss of wakefulness.
asymmetry
A characteristic of possible skin cancer in 
which one half or side of a mole does not 
match the other half or side.
best supportive care
Health care for the symptoms of cancer and 
the side effects of cancer treatment. Also 
called palliative care.
biopsy
Removal of small amounts of tissue from your 
body to test for cancer.
BRAF mutations
BRAF mutations can cause normal cells to 
become cancerous. They are most commonly 
found in melanomas but can occur in other 
forms of cancer.
Breslow thickness
A measure of how deep the melanoma tumor 
has grown into the skin.
broad-spectrum sunscreen
A substance that protects the skin from the sun 
by blocking 2 types of harmful ultraviolet (UV) 
rays—UVA and UVB.
cancer stage
Rating or description of the growth and spread 
of cancer in the body.
chemotherapy
Drugs that kill fast-growing cells, including 
normal cells and cancer cells.
clinical stage
A rating of the extent of melanoma in the body 
based on the physical exam and biopsy of the 
first (primary) tumor.
clinical trial
Research on a test or treatment to assess its 
safety or how well it works.
complete lymph node dissection (CLND)
A type of surgery that removes the remaining 
lymph nodes after a positive sentinel lymph 
node biopsy. Also called completion lymph 
node dissection.
computed tomography (CT) scan
An imaging scan that uses x-rays from many 
angles to make detailed pictures of the inside 
of the body.
contrast
A substance put into the body to make clearer 
pictures during imaging.
dermal mitotic rate (DMR)
A measure of how many cancer cells are 
growing and dividing in the dermis.

dermatologist
A doctor who’s an expert in skin diseases.
dermis
The second layer of skin beneath the top layer

distant metastasis
Spread of cancer cells to a part of the body far

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)
A long molecule that contains our unique

epidermis
The outer layer of skin.
excision
Removal by surgery.

Surgery that removes the entire skin tumor

fine-needle aspiration
Use of a thin needle to remove fluid or tissue

follow-up tests
Tests done after treatment to check for signs

general anesthesia
A drug-induced, sleep-like state for pain relief.
genes
A set of coded instructions in cells for making 
new cells and controlling how cells behave.
hypodermis
Deep skin tissue made of subcutaneous fat,

vessels.
imaging
Any radiology test that makes pictures 
(images) of the inside of the body.
immunotherapy
Treatment that uses the immune 
system to kill cancer cells.
infusion
A method of putting fluids, including drugs, into

in-transit metastases
The spread of skin cancer through a lymph

lentigo maligna
A slow-growing, early-stage melanoma often

lymphedema
Swelling due to buildup of lymph fluid in the

site.
lymph node
A small, disease-fighting structure located 
throughout the body.
lymph node basin
A group of lymph nodes that receive and filter 
lymph that flows from a certain area of the 
body.
lymph vessels
Similar to blood vessels, lymph vessels are 
thin-walled and tube-like but carry a fluid called 
lymph.
metastasis
Spread of cancer cells from the first (primary) 
tumor to a new site.

Words to know
microsatellites
Tiny tumor deposits that have spread to 
lymph vessels in the skin near the first 
melanoma tumor and can only be seen 
with a microscope.
neoadjuvant treatment
Treatment (usually medicine) given before the 
main (primary) treatment, usually surgery.
palliative care
Specialized medical care aimed at increasing 
quality of life and reducing pain and discomfort 
for people with serious, complex illnesses.
radiation therapy
Treatment that uses high-energy rays to kill 
cancer cells.
recurrence
Return of cancer after a cancer-free period.
resectable
Cancer able to be removed (resected) by 
surgery.
sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB)
Surgery to find and remove a sentinel lymph

stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)
Treatment that gives a single, large dose

limiting the dose to surrounding organs.
sun protection factor (SPF)
A number that indicates how well the

against sunburn.
talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC)
Treatment that uses a virus to infect and kill 
cancer cells while avoiding normal, healthy 
cells.
targeted therapy
Drug treatment that identifies and attacks a 
specific feature of cancer cells with less harm 
to normal cells.
tumor
An abnormal mass of tissue from cells growing 
or dividing too much that can be benign (not 
cancer) or cancer. In melanoma, a lesion or a 
spot is also called a tumor.
tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy
Treatment where tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes 
are removed from the person's tumor, grown in 
a laboratory, and given back to the person by 
infusion.
ulceration
A break in the skin like an open sore.
unresectable
Cancer that can't be surgically removed.
